The disc as a load spreader HYPOTHESIS

Back pain is often blamed on “instability”: a spine that moves too much. Bob Mulholland, a spine surgeon in Nottingham, argued in 2008 that this is a myth. In his view the real problem is how load passes through a damaged disc (Mulholland 2008, European Spine Journal).

  • A healthy disc behaves like a fluid-filled cushion, spreading load evenly across the bone above and below.
  • A worn disc loses that. Load becomes patchy, with high-pressure spots, often only in certain positions.
  • The evidence he points to: pressure measured inside patients’ discs found the painful ones were those where the centre carried no load, or where load was concentrated in small spots. Other worn discs weren’t painful.
  • Positions, not movement: he notes that people with chronic back pain describe pain in held postures, such as sitting, bending and leaning forward, rather than from moving.
  • Surgery: making spinal fusions ever more rigid didn’t improve pain results, which he takes as a sign that stopping movement isn’t the point.

It’s a strongly argued view from one surgeon and his colleagues, not settled consensus.

Why it matters for a flat back: it’s one reason the running cues focus on landing softly, and the sitting series on the positions I hold, not just how I move.

Flat backs and disc wear EVIDENCE HYPOTHESIS

A few studies link the flat-back shape to disc problems. They’re small, and they show links, not cause.

My working hypothesis: a flat shape may make some discs wear sooner, and once worn they may hurt through uneven loading in particular positions. Neither step is proven, and neither says anything certain about any one person’s back.

Why sitting hurts EVIDENCE MY REASONING

Mulholland cites a 2003 study by Maigne and colleagues: people whose back pain came on when sitting and eased when standing tended to have marked loss of disc height at the front of the disc. He reads sitting pain as flexion pain, from loading the front of the disc (Mulholland 2008).

That fits my own pattern: sciatica within minutes of sitting, eased by lying down or walking. It’s also the reasoning behind the floating chair: a seat tilted 5–10° forward lets the pelvis roll upright instead of tucking under, which should take some load off the front of the disc. That last step is my reasoning and hasn’t been tested.
